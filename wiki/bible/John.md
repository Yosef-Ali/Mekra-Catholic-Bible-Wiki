## Overview

የዮሐንስ ወንጌል (The Gospel according to John) stands apart from Matthew, Mark, and Luke — the "Synoptic" Gospels — in structure, style, and theological depth. Where the Synoptics recount Jesus' Galilean ministry through parables and exorcisms, John presents long discourses, meditative dialogues, and seven great "signs" (σημεῖα) that reveal Jesus' divine identity. Early tradition identifies the author as John the apostle, "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (13:23; 21:20–24), writing in Ephesus toward the end of the first century (c. AD 90–100). The Gospel is the product of decades of theological reflection within the Johannine community, and it opens with the most sublime Christological statement in Scripture: the eternal Word (ቃል/Λόγος) who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, became flesh and dwelled among us (1:1, 14).

John's stated purpose is evangelistic and life-giving: "these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name" (20:31). The Gospel is structured in two major sections: the Book of Signs (chs. 1–12), where Jesus reveals His glory through seven miracles (changing water to wine, healing the official's son, healing the paralytic, feeding the 5,000, walking on water, healing the man born blind, and raising Lazarus), and the Book of Glory (chs. 13–20), which begins with the Last Supper discourse and proceeds through the Passion, Resurrection, and appearances. The Farewell Discourse (chs. 14–17) is the theological heart of the Gospel, where Jesus promises the coming Paraclete (Holy Spirit) and prays for the unity of all believers.

## Key Themes

**The Divinity of Christ and the "I AM" Statements.** John's Christology is the most exalted in the New Testament. The Prologue (1:1–18) declares the pre-existence, divinity, and incarnation of the Word with language that echoes Genesis 1:1. Throughout the Gospel, Jesus uses the divine name "I AM" (ἐγώ εἰμι / እኔ ነኝ) in seven great declarations: "I am the Bread of Life" (6:35), "I am the Light of the World" (8:12), "I am the Gate" (10:9), "I am the Good Shepherd" (10:11), "I am the Resurrection and the Life" (11:25), "I am the Way, the Truth, and the Life" (14:6), and "I am the True Vine" (15:1). These echo the divine self-disclosure in Exodus 3:14 ("I AM WHO I AM") and constitute an unambiguous claim to divinity — which is precisely why Jesus' opponents take up stones (8:59). This profoundly sacramentally and doctrinally shapes Catholic teaching on the Incarnation and the Trinity.

**Eternal Life through Faith.** For John, "eternal life" (የዘላለም ሕይወት / ζωὴ αἰώνιος) is not merely a future promise but a present possession of those who believe in Jesus (3:36, 5:24). Faith is not intellectual assent alone but a living relationship — expressed in the language of "abiding" (μένειν): believers abide in Christ as branches in the vine (15:1–8). The Gospel's most famous verse, John 3:16, encapsulates the entire economy of salvation: the Father's love sends the Son so that believers might not perish but have eternal life. The sacramental dimension of this faith is particularly strong in John: the discourse on the Bread of Life (ch. 6) is the foundation of Eucharistic theology, the promise of living water (4:14, 7:37–39) points to Baptism and the Spirit, and the blood and water flowing from Christ's side (19:34) have been read by the Fathers as the birth of the Church and her sacraments.

**The Holy Spirit as Paraclete.** John provides the most developed New Testament teaching on the person and work of the Holy Spirit. In the Farewell Discourse, Jesus promises to send "another Advocate" (Παράκλητος / መናገሻ — Comforter, Counselor) who will be with the disciples forever (14:16). The Spirit is the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father (15:26), teaches all things (14:26), bears witness to Christ (15:26), convicts the world of sin, righteousness, and judgment (16:8–11), and guides the Church into all truth (16:13). At the resurrection, Jesus breathes on the apostles and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (20:22) — a Johannine Pentecost that the Compendium connects both to the institution of the sacrament of Penance and to the theology of Confirmation.

## Key Passages

> «በመጀመሪያ ቃል ነበረ፤ ቃልም በእግዚአብሔር ዘንድ ነበረ፤ ቃልም እግዚአብሔር ነበረ።»
> — ዮሐንስ 1:1

The sublime opening of the Prologue — the eternal Word is both with God and is God, the foundation of Trinitarian and Christological doctrine.

> «"በእርሱ የሚያምን ሁሉ የዘላለም ሕይወት እንዲኖረው እንጂ እንዳይጠፋ እግዚአብሔር አንድያ ልጁን እስኪሰጥ ድረስ ዓለሙን እንዲሁ ወዶአልና።»
> — ዮሐንስ 3:16

The Gospel in miniature — the Father's love, the gift of the Son, and the promise of eternal life to all who believe.

> «ኢየሱስም እንዲህ አላቸው "የሕይወት እንጀራ እኔ ነኝ፤ ወደ እኔ የሚመጣ ከቶ አይራብም፤ በእኔ የሚያምንም በፍጹም አይጠማም።»
> — ዮሐንስ 6:35

The first "I AM" statement — Jesus as the Bread of Life, the foundation of Eucharistic theology developed in the Bread of Life discourse.

> «ኢየሱስም "እኔ መንገድና እውነት ሕይወትም ነኝ፤ በእኔ በኩል ካልሆነ ማንም ወደ አብ የሚመጣ የለም።»
> — ዮሐንስ 14:6

The exclusive and universal claim — Christ is the sole mediator between humanity and the Father, the heart of Catholic soteriology.

> «ነገር ግን ኢየሱስ መሢሑ፤ የእግዚአብሔር ልጅ እንደሆነ ታምኑ ዘንድ፤ አምናችሁም በስሙ ሕይወት ይሆንላችሁ ዘንድ ይህ ተጽፏል።»
> — ዮሐንስ 20:31

The Gospel's stated purpose — the whole work is written to engender faith in Jesus as Messiah and Son of God, and through that faith to grant life.
