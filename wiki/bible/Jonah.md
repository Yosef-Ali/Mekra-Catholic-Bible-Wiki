## Overview

ትንቢተ ዮናስ (The Book of Jonah) is unique among the prophetic books: it contains almost no prophecy — only five Hebrew words of oracle ("Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown," 3:4) — and is instead a narrative about a reluctant prophet. Jonah ben Amittai is called to preach repentance to Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire, Israel's mortal enemy. He flees in the opposite direction, is swallowed by a great fish, prays from its belly, is delivered, finally preaches to Nineveh, and then — when the entire city repents and God relents — is furious at God's mercy. The book is a satire on narrow nationalism and a profound meditation on the universality of God's mercy. Jesus makes Jonah the primary sign of His own death and resurrection: "For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matt 12:40). He also contrasts the repentance of Nineveh at Jonah's preaching with the unbelief of His own generation (Matt 12:41). Jonah is read in its entirety at the Jewish afternoon service of Yom Kippur as the great liturgy of repentance and divine mercy.

## Key Themes

**Universal Salvation and the Scandal of Mercy.** The central theological message of Jonah is that God's mercy extends to all peoples — even to Israel's enemies, even to the brutal Assyrians. Jonah 4:2 is the theological climax: Jonah confesses the creed of Exodus 34:6–7 — that God is "gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relents from disaster" — but he says it as an accusation. He fled to Tarshish precisely because he knew God would forgive the Ninevites, and he wanted them destroyed. The book confronts the reader with the question God poses to Jonah: "Should I not pity Nineveh?" (4:11). The answer is the universality of divine mercy — God cares for the 120,000 residents of Nineveh "who do not know their right hand from their left, and also much cattle." This radical universalism is foundational for [[teaching/god-the-father-creator]] and the Catholic understanding of God's universal salvific will.

**The Sign of Jonah — Death and Resurrection.** Jesus makes Jonah the primary Old Testament type of His death and resurrection. Jonah's three days in the belly of the fish prefigure Christ's three days in the tomb. Jonah's "prayer from the belly of Sheol" (ch. 2) is the Old Testament's great psalm of descent and deliverance — "I went down to the land whose bars closed upon me forever; yet you brought up my life from the pit" (2:6). The Church reads Jonah as a type of Christ's descent into hell and His resurrection, directly supporting [[teaching/passion-death-resurrection]] and [[teaching/forgiveness-resurrection-eternal-life]].

**The Reluctant Prophet and the Obedience of Faith.** Jonah is not a hero but an anti-hero — the reluctant prophet who runs from God's call. His story is a profound exploration of the struggle between human will and divine mission. The pagan sailors (ch. 1) and the pagan Ninevites (ch. 3) emerge as more responsive to God than the Israelite prophet. The book subverts every expectation: the prophet disobeys, the pagans repent, and God's mercy extends to all. This narrative of vocation and resistance speaks powerfully to [[teaching/faith-and-revelation]] and the obedience of faith.

## Key Passages

> «በመከራዬ ሆኜ ጌታን ጠራሁት፤ እርሱም መለሰልኝ፤ በሲኦልም ሆድ ውስጥ ሆኜ ጮኸሁ፤ ቃልህንም ሰማሁ።»
> — ዮናስ 2:3

Jonah's prayer from the belly of the fish — the cry from Sheol that God hears. The Old Testament type of Christ's descent into the heart of the earth and His resurrection.

> «አንተ ቸርና ርኅሩኅ፣ ታጋሽና ምሕረትህ የበዛ፣ ከክፉ የምትመለስ አምላክ እንደሆንህ አውቅ ነበርና።»
> — ዮናስ 4:2

Jonah's confession — the creed of Exodus 34 spoken as accusation. Jonah knows God's mercy and resents it, revealing the scandal of universal grace.

> «እኔስ ታዲያ ቀኛቸውንና ግራቸውን የማይለዩ ከመቶ ሃያ ሺህ የሚበልጡ ሰዎችና ብዙ እንስሶች ላሉባት ለታላቂቱ ከተማ ለነነዌ አላዝንምን?»
> — ዮናስ 4:11

God's final question — the book ends open-ended, demanding the reader's answer: if you pity a plant, should not God pity a great city? The universal scope of divine mercy.
