## Overview

ኦሪት ዘዳግም (Deuteronomy, from the Greek δευτερονόμιον meaning "second law") is the fifth and final book of the Torah. Its Hebrew name, דְּבָרִים (Devarim, "words"), captures its literary form: a series of farewell speeches delivered by Moses to Israel on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, as the people prepare to enter the Promised Land without him. The book is structured around three major orations (chs. 1–4, 5–28, 29–30), followed by Moses' final blessings, the Song of Moses, and his death on Mount Nebo (chs. 31–34). Deuteronomy is not a mere repetition of the law but a covenant renewal document — Moses recapitulates the exodus and wilderness narrative, re-proclaims the Decalogue (ch. 5), and calls Israel to a wholehearted love of God expressed in obedience. The book is among the most-quoted Old Testament texts in the New Testament: Jesus answers Satan's temptations with Deuteronomy alone (Matthew 4:1–11), and the Shema (6:4–5) is identified by Jesus as the greatest commandment (Mark 12:29–30). Deuteronomy is the charter of covenant fidelity, a summons to choose life, and the theological bridge from the exodus generation to the life of Israel in the land.

## Key Themes

**The Shema and the Call to Wholehearted Love.** The Shema Israel ("Hear, O Israel," 6:4–5) is the theological and devotional heart of Deuteronomy — and arguably of the entire Old Testament. It begins with a confession of monotheism (the LORD alone is God) and moves immediately to the command to love God with one's entire being: heart, soul, and strength. This is no abstract theological proposition but a call to a total personal response of love, the foundation of the covenant relationship. The Shema is followed by the command to internalize these words — to teach them to children, bind them as a sign, and write them on doorposts (6:6–9) — indicating that the law is meant to shape every dimension of life, from the heart outward to home and community. Jesus identifies the Shema as the greatest commandment, adding Leviticus 19:18 (love of neighbor) as its inseparable corollary (Mark 12:28–34). This synthetic love command grounds [[teaching/first-three-commandments]] and [[teaching/moral-law-and-grace]], showing that the moral law flows from and is ordered to charity.

**Election and Covenant: A Chosen People for the Sake of the Nations.** Deuteronomy grounds Israel's identity not in its own merit but in God's gratuitous, sovereign love: "You are a people holy to the LORD your God... He has chosen you to be a people for His own possession" (7:6). This election is pure grace — not because Israel was the greatest or most righteous of nations, but because the LORD set His love upon them and kept the oath He swore to the fathers (7:7–8). Election creates obligation: Israel is called to holiness, obedience, and the remembrance of its redemption from Egypt. The covenant structure of Deuteronomy — with its blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience (chs. 27–28) — establishes the Deuteronomic theology of history that shapes the entire Deuteronomistic History (Joshua through Kings). This theology of election and covenant supports [[teaching/faith-and-revelation]] and [[teaching/the-church]], as the Church understands herself as the new People of God, chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world (Ephesians 1:4).

**The Prophet Like Moses, the Choice of Life, and Covenant Renewal.** Three theological pillars bring Deuteronomy to its climax. First, Moses promises that God will raise up a prophet like him from among Israel's brothers (18:15), a prophecy that the New Testament sees fulfilled in Christ — the new Moses who delivers not from Egypt but from sin, and who teaches with divine authority on the mountain (Matthew 5–7). Second, the great choice set before Israel at the end of the covenant renewal ceremony: "I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life" (30:19). This is not Pelagian self-salvation but a decision to live in the covenant relationship that God has already graciously established. Third, the renewal of the covenant on the plains of Moab (chs. 29–30) demonstrates that God's covenant is not a one-time event but a living relationship requiring continual ratification. This prophet-typology grounds [[teaching/jesus-christ-incarnation]], while the call to choose life undergirds Catholic moral teaching on free will and responsibility in [[teaching/moral-law-and-grace]].

## Key Passages

> «እስራኤል ሆይ፥ ስማ! ጌታ አምላካችን አንድ ጌታ ነው። አንተም ጌታ አምላክህን በፍጹም ልብህ፥ በፍጹም ነፍስህና በፍጹም ኃይልህ ውደድ።»
> — ዘዳግም 6:4–5

The Shema Israel — the foundational confession of Israel's faith, the command to love God totally, identified by Jesus as the greatest commandment.

> «ለአምላክህ ለጌታ አንተ ቅዱስ ሕዝብ ነህና፤ በምድር ላይ ካሉት ሕዝቦች ሁሉ፤ ጌታ አምላክህ ለእርሱ የተለየ ሕዝብ እንድትሆን መርጦሃል።»
> — ዘዳግም 7:6

The election of Israel — a holy people, chosen and set apart by God's gratuitous love, the Old Testament root of the theology of the Church as the new People of God.

> «አምላክህ ጌታ ከአንተ ወንድሞችህ መካከል እንደ እኔ ያለ ነቢይ ያስነሣልሃል፤ እርሱን ስሙት።»
> — ዘዳግም 18:15

The promise of a prophet like Moses — read Christologically as the promise of the new and greater Moses, fulfilled in Jesus the teacher and deliverer.

> «በሕይወትና በሞት፤ በበረከትና መርገም በፊትህ እንዳስቀመጥኩ ዛሬ ሰማይንና ምድርን ምስክር አድርጌ እጠራብሃለሁ። እንግዲህ አንተና ልጆችህ በሕይወት እንድትኖሩ ሕይወትን ምረጡ፤»
> — ዘዳግም 30:19

The great choice — life and death set before God's people, a summons to covenant fidelity that respects human freedom and calls for a decisive response.
