## Overview

ትንቢተ ኤርምያስ (The Book of Jeremiah) is the longest prophetic book in the Old Testament (by word count) and the most intense personal portrait of a prophet's interior life. Jeremiah ben Hilkiah, of the priestly line of Anathoth, received his call as a youth in 627 BC and prophesied for over forty years through the final decades of the kingdom of Judah — the reforms of Josiah, the collapse under Jehoiakim and Zedekiah, the Babylonian siege, the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple in 587 BC, and the aftermath in Egypt. The book is a complex weave of poetic oracles, prose sermons, biographical narratives (from Baruch the scribe), and the deeply personal "Confessions of Jeremiah" — laments in which the prophet wrestles with God over his vocation, his suffering, and the apparent failure of his mission. Jeremiah is nicknamed "the weeping prophet," but his tears are the tears of God over His faithless bride, Israel. Above all, Jeremiah is the prophet of the new covenant — the promise that God will write His law on human hearts, a promise the Church sees fulfilled in Christ and celebrated in every Eucharist.

> የኬልቅያስ ልጅ ኤርምያስ የካህናት ወገን ከሆነ ቤተሰብ፤ በቢንያም ግዛት በምትገኝ በዓናቶት በምትባል በአነስተኛ ከተማ ተወለደ። ኤርምያስ የነቢይነቱን አገልግሎት የጀመረው በኢዮስያስ በነገሠ በዓሥራ ሦስተኛው ዓመት 627 ከክርስቶስ ልደት በፊት ነበር። አገልግሎቱንም ያበቃው ባቢሎናውያን ኢየሩሳሌምን በ 587 ## Key Themes

**Prophetic Call and the Interior Cost of Vocation.** Jeremiah's call narrative (1:4–10) is one of the most profound in Scripture: before God formed him in the womb, He knew him, consecrated him, and appointed him a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah's protest — "I am only a youth" — is overruled by the divine imperative: "To all to whom I send you, you shall go." This election from the womb establishes that the prophetic vocation is not a career choice but an ontological reality rooted in God's prior knowledge and purpose. The Confessions of Jeremiah (chs. 11–20) reveal the interior cost of this vocation: the prophet is isolated, mocked, imprisoned, and thrown into a cistern. He accuses God of deceiving him (20:7) and curses the day of his birth (20:14–18), yet cannot suppress the word that burns like fire in his bones (20:9). Jeremiah's vocation prefigures Christ's — the prophet rejected by His own, and the pattern of [[teaching/faith-and-revelation]], where faith is not ease but trust in God's word through suffering.

**The New Covenant: Law Written on the Heart.** The oracle of the new covenant (31:31–34) is the theological summit of Jeremiah and one of the most important passages in the entire Old Testament. Unlike the Mosaic covenant broken by Israel, the new covenant will be interior: God will put His law within them and write it on their hearts. It will involve an immediate, personal knowledge of God ("they shall all know me, from the least to the greatest") and a definitive forgiveness of sins. This is the longest Old Testament text quoted in the New Testament — Hebrews 8:8–12 and 10:16–17 cite it in full — and the institution of the Eucharist is explicitly framed as the blood of the new covenant (Luke 22:20, 1 Cor 11:25). Jeremiah 31 thus directly grounds [[teaching/eucharist]], [[teaching/the-church]] as the community of the new covenant, and [[teaching/moral-law-and-grace]] — the moral life is not external conformity but the fruit of a transformed heart. The promise of definitive forgiveness supports [[teaching/penance]].

**Hope in Exile, Judgment, and Restoration.** Jeremiah's most surprising prophetic act is his letter to the exiles in Babylon (ch. 29): instead of promising immediate return, he commands them to build houses, plant gardens, marry, and seek the welfare of the city — "for in its welfare you will find your welfare" (29:7). The famous verse "I know the plans I have for you, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (29:11) is not a promise of pain-free prosperity but of covenant faithfulness through the long discipline of exile. Jeremiah balances unsparing judgment — the Temple sermon (ch. 7) denounces the superstitious trust in the Temple as a talisman while the people practice injustice — with promises of restoration: a righteous Branch from David's line (23:5–6, 33:15–16), a return from exile, and the rebuilding of the city. This dialectic of judgment and hope shapes [[teaching/sin]] and the Catholic understanding of penitence, and the "righteous Branch" supports [[teaching/jesus-christ-incarnation]].

## Key Passages

> «በማኅፀን ውስጥ ሳልሠራህ በፊት አውቄሃለሁ፤ ከማኅፀንም ሳትወጣ ቀድሼሃለሁ፤ ለአሕዛብም ነቢይ አድርጌ ሾሜሃለሁ።»
> — ኤርምያስ 1:5

The call of Jeremiah — known, consecrated, and appointed from the womb, the paradigm of prophetic vocation rooted in divine election before birth.

> «እነሆ፥ ከእስራኤል ቤትና ከይሁዳ ቤት ጋር አዲስ ቃል ኪዳን የምገባበት ወራት ይመጣል፥ ይላል ጌታ፤»
> — ኤርምያስ 31:31

The announcement of the new covenant — not like the Mosaic covenant that Israel broke, but a covenant written on the heart, fulfilled in Christ and celebrated in the Eucharist.

> «ሕጌን በልባቸው አኖራለሁ፥ በልባቸውም እጽፋለሁ፤ እኔም አምላክ እሆናቸዋለሁ እነርሱም ሕዝብ ይሆኑኛል።»
> — ኤርምያስ 31:33

The interiority of the new covenant — the law written on the heart, immediate knowledge of God, and definitive forgiveness, the charter of the Church.

> «ለእናንተ የማስባትን አሳብ እኔ አውቃለሁ፤ ይላል ጌታ፤ ለወደፊት ተስፋ እሰጣችሁ ዘንድ የሰላም አሳብ ነው እንጂ የክፉ ነገር አይደለም።»
> — ኤርምያስ 29:11

God's plans for the exiles — plans for shalom (peace, welfare, wholeness), a hope anchored not in immediate rescue but in the long faithfulness of God through exile.
