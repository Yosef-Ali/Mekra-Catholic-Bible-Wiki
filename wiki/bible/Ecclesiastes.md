## Overview

Ecclesiastes (መጽሐፈ መክብብ, "The Book of the Preacher/Assembler") is the Bible's most unflinching meditation on the limits of human wisdom and the vanity of life "under the sun." Attributed to Qoheleth (the "Preacher," traditionally identified with Solomon in his old age), the book relentlessly interrogates every source of human meaning — wisdom, pleasure, work, wealth, power, even righteousness — and finds each one insufficient to bear the weight of eternity. Its famous refrain, "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity" (1:2), sets the tone for a work that has been called the most philosophical book in Scripture and the most honest.

Yet Ecclesiastes is not a counsel of despair. The Preacher's honesty about life's futility is the necessary prelude to genuine faith — stripping away every idol so that the fear of God remains as the only solid ground. The book's final word (12:13–14) is not nihilistic but reverent: "Fear God and keep His commandments, for this is the whole duty of man." Catholic tradition reads Ecclesiastes as a diagnosis of the world's vanity apart from God, a diagnosis that finds its cure in the resurrection of Christ — the one who has conquered death and given eternal meaning to temporal life. The Catechism draws on Ecclesiastes in its treatment of the desire for God (CCC 27–30) and the reality of death (CCC 1006–1009). The Amharic title መጽሐፈ መክብብ means "The Book of the Assembler/Preacher," reflecting the Hebrew Qoheleth.

## Key Themes

**The Vanity of Life "Under the Sun."** Qoheleth's key term is hevel (translated as "vanity" or "emptiness") — a word that literally means "vapor" or "breath," something fleeting, insubstantial, and impossible to grasp. He applies this verdict to wisdom (it cannot ultimately solve the riddle of existence), to pleasure (it dissipates and leaves emptiness), to work and achievement (you die and leave it to another), to wealth (it cannot buy meaning), and even to righteousness (the wicked prosper and the righteous suffer, and all die alike). The phrase "under the sun" — used 29 times — defines the horizon of a world considered without reference to God's redemptive action, a world bounded by birth and death with no escape. This is not the final biblical word but a necessary purgation: Ecclesiastes forces the reader to confront the insufficiency of every created good, preparing the way for the revelation that meaning comes not from comprehending life but from receiving it as gift. This diagnosis of worldly vanity supports [[teaching/man-and-the-fall]] and [[teaching/sin]], and the whole Catholic theology of detachment.

**The Gift of Temporal Joy.** Paradoxically, the book that declares everything vanity also contains the Bible's most repeated exhortation to enjoy life: eat, drink, and find satisfaction in one's toil, for this is the gift of God (2:24, 3:13, 5:18, 8:15, 9:7–10). Qoheleth is no ascetic who despises creation; he insists that temporal joys — good food, honest work, loving companionship — are real goods given by God. They simply cannot bear the weight of ultimate meaning. This tension between honest enjoyment and honest acknowledgment of limits is a distinctively biblical posture: neither hedonism nor world-denial but grateful reception of the present moment as divine gift. This dimension of Ecclesiastes supports [[teaching/god-the-father-creator]] — creation is good and to be enjoyed — while guarding against making created goods into idols.

**The Conclusion: Fear God and Keep His Commandments.** After twelve chapters of relentless questioning, the book's epilogue (12:9–14) delivers its verdict with startling simplicity: "Fear God and keep His commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every deed into judgment." The Preacher does not resolve the intellectual problems he has raised — he simply acknowledges the sovereignty of God and the moral seriousness of human life. This is wisdom at its limit: not a solution to the problem of meaning but a posture of trust in the God who holds all deeds in His hands. Catholic tradition sees here the foundation of the theological virtue of hope — not optimism based on evidence but trust in God's future judgment that will set all things right. This conclusion grounds [[teaching/faith-and-revelation]] and [[teaching/forgiveness-resurrection-eternal-life]] — the final judgment is the horizon that gives meaning to the present.

## Key Passages

> «ሰባኪው፦ ከንቱ፤ ከንቱ፤ የከንቱ ከንቱ፤ ሁሉ ከንቱ ነው ይላል፡፡»
> — መክብብ 1:2

The opening salvo — the Preacher's verdict on life considered apart from God, the refrain that echoes through the entire book like a funeral bell.

> «ለሁሉ ዘመን አለው፤ ከሰማይ በታችም ለሆነ ነገር ሁሉ ጊዜ አለው። ለመውለድ ጊዜ አለው፤ ለመሞትም ጊዜ አለው ... ለመውደድ ጊዜ አለው፤ ለመጥላትም ጊዜ አለው፤ ለጦርነት ጊዜ አለው፤ ለሰላምም ጊዜ አለው።»
> — መክብብ 3:1-2, 8

The poem on times and seasons — the Bible's most celebrated meditation on the rhythm of human existence under divine sovereignty, a poetic recognition that we are not masters of our time.

> «የነገሩ ሁሉ ፍጻሜ እንስማ፤ ይህ የሰው ሁለንተናው ነውና፤ እግዚአብሔርን ፍራ፥ ትእዛዙንም ጠብቅ።»
> — መክብብ 12:13

The final word — after all the questioning, the conclusion is not an intellectual solution but a posture of reverence and obedience before the God who will judge all.
